The premise.

DDD already moves encrypted bundles between disconnected phones and the internet using buses, mules, and anyone going somewhere. The drop box adds a patient, stationary node to that network.

Today, a villager's phone exchanges with a bus driver's phone via Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware whenever they happen to be in the same place at the same time. This works, but depends on a real-time rendezvous: the bus has to pull in while the villager is at the stop, both phones awake and discoverable.

A stationary phone parked at the bus stop solves this asymmetry. Villagers exchange with it whenever they pass — early morning, late evening, between errands. Bus drivers exchange with it when they pull in. Neither needs to be there at the same time. The drop box becomes the patient memory of the transport network.

01

It's just a stationary BundleTransport

From the protocol's perspective, the drop box is another transport. It runs the same BundleTransport service, speaks the same gRPC over Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware, sees only ciphertext it cannot decrypt. The cryptography and trust model don't change — the drop box is just a transport that doesn't move and never sleeps. More in §02 of Full design.

02

The hardware is a $25 used Android phone

A retired Tecno, BLU, or Itel — abundant in Sub-Saharan African second-hand markets — runs the existing transport code with minimal changes. Routers and Raspberry Pis sound appealing but they don't speak Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware peer-to-peer, which would break the seamless discovery the system depends on. ESP32 microcontrollers do support Wi-Fi Aware natively but can't run our Java-based bundle and cryptography stack — porting it to embedded C is a research project, not a near-term deployment option. Hardware comparison in §03.

03

Software changes are small and additive

The existing BundleTransportService is already a long-running foreground service that survives reboots. Drop box mode adds: a wake lock, battery optimization whitelisting, screen-off operation, two new fields (type=dropbox, location) in the DNS-SD service announcement, and storage quota management. Optional but valuable second pass: dual-protocol Wi-Fi Aware support alongside Wi-Fi Direct, using the Trick research project as a reference. Priorities in §04.

04

Power is solved cheaply

Screen off, the phone draws roughly half a watt as a Wi-Fi Direct group owner — about as much as an LED night light. A 5 W solar panel of the kind already sold across African markets for solar lanterns, plus a small buffer cell, runs the device indefinitely. For disaster response, a 20,000 mAh power bank lasts ~40 hours and gets swapped on the bus driver's regular route. Power and physical design in §06.

05

Around $60 per deployed unit

The complete pilot configuration — used phone, microSD, 3D-printed PETG enclosure, power bank, mounting hardware — lands at $50–62 per drop box. A self-sufficient solar version is $58–78. Adding Wi-Fi Aware support requires a Qualcomm-chipset phone (budget MediaTek devices typically lack the driver enablement), bringing the cost to $88–125. Full cost breakdown in §08.

06

The drop box is application-agnostic

Email is the first DDD application — a fork of K-9 Mail talks to the bundle system through a standard ContentProvider IPC. Future applications (messaging, content delivery, games) use the same pattern with their own appId. The drop box stores opaque encrypted bundles regardless of what's inside. There's no email-specific logic anywhere in the drop box — by design. More in §09.

§ 01

The problem we are solving

Range, timing, and the gap between "transport passing through" and "villager wants to send"
Recap. DDD moves end-to-end encrypted bundles from disconnected phones to the internet over physical carriers — buses, mules, anyone going somewhere. First app: email, via a K-9 fork.

DDD's transport layer assumes that a moving carrier — typically a bus driver — is in proximity to a villager's phone for long enough that an exchange completes over Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware. Both radios have a usable range of roughly 50–100 meters in line-of-sight, considerably less indoors. This works in theory. In practice it requires three things to align: the transport is present, the villager is present, and the villager's phone is awake and discoverable at the right moment.

The current system handles this by retrying on subsequent encounters and by accepting that not every encounter completes a full exchange. But this puts real pressure on encounter frequency. A bus passing through a remote village twice a day, for fifteen minutes each, gives at most thirty minutes of opportunity per day — and only if every villager who needs to exchange happens to be at the stop during one of those windows.

A stationary device parked at the stop solves this asymmetry. The villager exchanges with it whenever they pass — early morning, late evening, between errands. The bus driver exchanges with it when they pull in. Neither needs to be there at the same time. The drop box becomes, in effect, the patient memory of the transport network — turning a brittle real-time rendezvous into a reliable store-and-forward queue.

Why now

Three things make this the right moment. The existing BundleTransport service is already a long-running Android service that survives reboots, manages a Wi-Fi Direct group owner, and runs the gRPC exchange protocol. It is — minus a few power and lifecycle hardening passes — already most of the way to being a stationary device. The Trick research project has shipped a working Wi-Fi Aware implementation that we can study as a reference. And the K-9 email fork has demonstrated that real applications can ride this transport, which makes the case for permanent infrastructure rather than experiments.

§ 02

Where the drop box fits

A new node between client and transport, but transparent to either
Key claim. The drop box adds a hop without changing the protocol. From the bundle's point of view, it has been carried by one transport instead of two.

From the cryptographic and protocol layers, the drop box is just another BundleTransport. It speaks the same gRPC service, advertises itself the same way over DNS-SD, runs the same exchange logic. What makes it a drop box is operational: it never moves, it has guaranteed power, it stays online, and it has more storage than any phone in someone's pocket would.

This is the deliberate design choice. We are not introducing a new role into the DDD trust model — the drop box sees only ciphertext, just like every other transport. Its only privileged position is geographic.

Fig. 01 The drop box sits between villagers and the bus driver transport. Bundles are end-to-end encrypted at every hop; the drop box holds ciphertext it cannot read, exactly like any other transport. The chain becomes client → drop box → transport → server.
Routing impact
The two-hop path is the one piece of the protocol that needs careful thought — see § 07.

The drop box doesn't change what bundles look like, what encryption they use, how they're acknowledged, or how the windowing system works. What it changes is the assumption baked into BundleRouting.processClientMetaData() on the server — that a transport's encounters with clients are direct. With a drop box in the chain, the server learns "transport X picked up bundles that originated with clients A, B, C" but the encounter itself happened at the drop box, not in the village. Most of the time this distinction doesn't matter. When it does — for routing return bundles back through the right transport — the metadata needs to tell that story.

§ 03

Hardware: why a phone wins

Routers and single-board computers don't speak the right protocols. Microcontrollers do, but can't run our software stack. The phone solves both.
The constraint that decides this.
Wi-Fi Direct and Wi-Fi Aware are intentionally peer-to-peer. They do not behave like a normal access point.

The natural instinct is to reach for purpose-built hardware — a small Linux router, a Raspberry Pi, a microcontroller with an SD card. Each is cheaper than a phone in raw component cost. But they each fail on a different axis. Routers and single-board computers can only offer a standard Wi-Fi access point, not Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware — which means they break the seamless peer discovery our existing apps rely on. Microcontrollers like the ESP32 actually do support Wi-Fi Aware natively8, so the protocol problem doesn't apply — but they have no JVM, no port of the Signal cryptography library, and the bundle format would need to be reimplemented in firmware from scratch.

Wi-Fi Direct and Wi-Fi Aware are designed exactly for the property we want — devices automatically discover one another in proximity, with no access point, no SSID to type, no password. This is what makes the current DDD experience seamless. A villager walks past a transport and the exchange happens. They don't think about it.

A standard Wi-Fi access point — which is all a router or Pi can offer to phones — breaks this. The phone has to leave its current network, join a new one, possibly accept a "no internet on this connection" warning, do the exchange, and reconnect. Android makes this progressively harder for legitimate privacy and battery reasons. Even with aggressive engineering, the experience would be worse than it is today, and the work to make the existing apps tolerate it is non-trivial.

The phone solution sidesteps all of this. A retired Tecno or BLU running the existing BundleTransport code is, by construction, a peer that other phones already know how to find.

The other reasons the phone wins are practical rather than technical. Phones in this class are already in the supply chain in the target deployment regions — Tecno and Itel are the dominant brands across Sub-Saharan Africa, with active second-hand markets and local repair shops in every market town. A drop box that fails can be replaced or repaired by anyone who can swap a SIM card. A drop box that needs a soldering iron and a Linux laptop cannot.

What about an antenna?

This question comes up reasonably often, so it's worth being precise. You cannot extend a phone's Wi-Fi range by attaching an external antenna — the antenna is etched onto the phone's mainboard and there is no exposed feed point. Any "boosters" you can buy that claim otherwise are at best useless and at worst harmful (they can confuse the impedance matching of the internal radio).

What does work, mechanically, is mounting the phone higher. Wi-Fi range falls off sharply as the line-of-sight clears human and structural obstacles. A phone at 3–4 meters covers meaningfully more area than one at 1 meter. A passive aluminum reflector behind the phone — a curved sheet, not a precisely tuned element — directs more energy in one direction and gives a real 3–5 dB advantage at the cost of a pole-clamp-sized piece of metal. For a bus stop where you know which direction the waiting area is, this is essentially free range.

§ 04

Software implementation

Five priorities, ordered by what unlocks the most value with the least invasive change
Good news. The existing BundleTransport code is already most of the way to being a drop box. It runs as a foreground service with START_STICKY, manages a Wi-Fi Direct group owner, runs a gRPC server, and survives reboots.

The implementation work is not "build a new app." It is "harden the existing transport service so it can run unattended forever, and add the small things that make a stationary node distinguishable from a moving one." The five priorities below are ordered to deliver the most value first. Items 1 and 2 together produce a working drop box. Items 3–5 improve the experience and unlock multi-transport scenarios.

01
High

Drop box mode in BundleTransportService

A single SharedPreferences flag, dropbox_mode = true, that on enable acquires a PARTIAL_WAKE_LOCK (keeps the CPU running while the screen stays off), programmatically requests battery-optimization whitelisting, and ensures no foreground activity is holding FLAG_KEEP_SCREEN_ON. The service already uses START_STICKY and auto-restarts after kills, so survival across reboots and OOM events is already handled. Add storage quota management — when the bundle store crosses 80% full, invoke StorageManager.updateStorage() to delete oldest. Extend the DNS-SD TXT record with type=dropbox and an optional location string so clients can show "Drop Box · Nairobi Bus Stop 3" instead of an opaque transport ID.

High

Dual-protocol discovery — Wi-Fi Aware alongside Wi-Fi Direct

Add Wi-Fi Aware publish/subscribe to BundleTransportService alongside the existing Wi-Fi Direct group owner. Check FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE at startup; if present, run both; if absent, Wi-Fi Direct alone is fully functional. Use Trick's AndroidWifiAwareManager as the reference for session management — attach, simultaneous publish + subscribe, network specifier, role negotiation. The exchange protocol (gRPC over TLS) is identical regardless of how the connection was discovered. Medium

Multi-transport bundle pickup coordination

A drop box in a busy area accumulates bundles from many villagers and hands them to whichever bus driver passes through. Today there is no per-transport tracking of "which bundles has this transport already taken." We need a small ledger inside the drop box: {transportId → [bundleIds downloaded]}. When a new transport arrives, prioritize bundles that no transport has confirmed delivery on, and offer already-handled ones as redundancy if there's still capacity. Confirmation flows through the existing ACK mechanism; for older bundles a heuristic — "N transports have taken it, deprioritize but don't delete" — handles the case where ACKs are slow to return.

05
Lower · Research

Two-hop routing metadata

The genuinely novel piece. Today, BundleRouting.processClientMetaData() assumes a single hop: the transport that delivered a bundle is the same one that received it from the client. With a drop box in the chain, the server needs to learn the two-hop path so it can route return bundles correctly. Drop box should include a small relay_metadata structure in its own bundle to the server — listing clients seen and transports that have picked up — and the server's routing service needs to handle relay entries. This is a real protocol extension worth framing as a research contribution.

The smallest change that makes a drop box visible as a drop box

Inside DDDWifiServer.createGroup(), the TXT record already advertises ddd, transportId, and the recency blob. Two new keys make a drop box self-identifying:

// DDDWifiServer.java — inside createGroup()
txt = new HashMap<>();
txt.put("ddd", bts.getBundleServerURL());
txt.put("transportId", bts.transportId);
txt.put("recencyBlob", Base64.getEncoder().encodeToString(lastRecencyBlob.toByteArray()));
txt.put("type",     bts.isDropBox() ? "dropbox" : "transport");
txt.put("location", bts.getDropBoxLocation());  // "Nairobi · Bus Stop 3"

The client side, on receiving a TXT record with type=dropbox, simply renders the peer with a different label and (if you want) prefers manual exchange over automatic background polling. That's the entire UI-layer change.

§ 05

Dual protocol — Aware when possible, Direct as fallback

The drop box advertises both. The connecting phone uses whichever it can.
Why both. Wi-Fi Aware is better when both ends support it. Wi-Fi Direct is universal. Most budget Android phones — the dominant device class in our target deployments — do not have Wi-Fi Aware. We need the fallback.

Wi-Fi Aware (Neighbor Awareness Networking, IEEE 802.11aq) is the better protocol. Its synchronized sleep/wake cycle keeps the radio off 96% of the time during idle discovery. It does not require giving up your Wi-Fi internet connection — the NAN interface is logically separate. It discovers peers in around a second versus several seconds for Wi-Fi Direct group owner negotiation. Multi-peer is native. From an engineering point of view, a future where everyone uses Wi-Fi Aware and Wi-Fi Direct is deprecated would be cleaner.

That future is not the present. Wi-Fi Aware requires the chipset to support it, the OEM to enable it in the driver, and the device to expose FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE. The exact phones our target population is most likely to own — Tecno, Itel, Infinix, generally MediaTek-based — frequently lack it even on otherwise capable hardware, because driver-level enablement is uneven. Qualcomm-based Android phones from 2018 onward generally have it. iPhones have it from iPhone 12 onward, but only on iOS 26+, and Apple's WiFiAware framework is brand new7.

Wi-Fi Direct, by contrast, is on every Android phone since version 4.0 and works without surprises. It's slower to set up, draws more power as group owner, and won't talk to iOS at all. But it works.

The drop box's job is to advertise both, accept either, and let the connecting client use whatever it has. If the drop box phone itself doesn't support Wi-Fi Aware, it falls back to Wi-Fi Direct only — still fully functional. This is in deliberate contrast to the Trick research project, which is Wi-Fi Aware only and shows an "Unsupported Device" screen if Aware isn't available — exactly the wrong model for our target population.

The ecosystem is converging on Wi-Fi Aware

One reason to invest in the Wi-Fi Aware path despite its current hardware unevenness: the ecosystem trajectory is clearly toward it. Apple was effectively required to ship Wi-Fi Aware in iOS 26 under the EU's Digital Markets Act, then released the framework worldwide12. Google shipped Quick Share ↔ AirDrop interoperability over Wi-Fi Aware on the Pixel 10 in November 2025, and announced expansion to many more Android phones throughout 202634. Matter 1.4.2 adopted Wi-Fi Aware Unsynchronized Service Discovery (NAN-USD) for commissioning5. The protocol is becoming the cross-platform standard that AWDL was for Apple alone.

This doesn't change the immediate implementation path — Wi-Fi Direct must remain the baseline because budget Android in our target deployments doesn't have Wi-Fi Aware yet, and developer-level cross-platform iOS↔Android NAN is still unreliable in practice (per Apple Developer Forums reports6). But it means the dual-protocol investment is durable: Wi-Fi Aware is becoming more, not less, supported over time.

§ 06

Power and physical design

A phone that runs forever needs surprisingly little — and the enclosure is more important than the panel
Honest baseline. Screen off, Wi-Fi Direct group owner active, gRPC server idle: ~0.5 W sustained. That's roughly the draw of a single LED night light.

The drop box's screen never needs to be on. There is no UI for a passerby — discovery happens through Wi-Fi protocols, exchange happens automatically. With the screen off and the radio in steady-state idle, an Android phone draws on the order of half a watt as a Wi-Fi Direct group owner, and as little as 0.2 W in Wi-Fi Aware idle. Active exchange is short bursts of 2–3 watts but only for the few seconds the radio is moving data.

Daily energy budget, screen off, conservative: about 14–24 Wh. A 5 W solar panel — the kind already sold across Sub-Saharan African markets for solar lanterns — generates roughly 25 Wh per day at 5–6 peak sun hours. That's enough to run the drop box continuously and charge a small buffer battery for nighttime operation.

For disaster response, where rapid deployment matters more than long-term self-sufficiency, a 20,000 mAh USB power bank does the job for 40+ hours and gets swapped on the bus driver's regular route. No solar required.

The thermal trap. A black enclosure in equatorial sun reaches 80°C+ inside. Phones throttle hard at 45°C and shut down at 60°C. Color choice is not aesthetic — it determines whether the device works.

The enclosure does more work than the panel

For a deployed drop box, the enclosure is where most of the deployment risk lives. The phone itself is solved — we know it works. The protocols are solved. The interesting failure modes are environmental.

Heat is the largest. A sealed black plastic box in direct equatorial sun will reach internal temperatures the phone cannot survive. White or light-gray PETG, ventilation slots with insect mesh, internal ribs that channel convection, and a small shade canopy above the enclosure all matter more than any individual radio choice.

Pressure is the second-largest. As the enclosure heats and cools through a daily cycle, the air inside expands and contracts. A perfectly sealed enclosure either pushes its lid off or, worse, pulls moist outside air in through the gasket on cooling. A small Gore-Tex membrane vent — about a dollar — solves this completely.

Water is the third. A weep hole at the lowest point of the enclosure with a fine mesh insert lets condensation drain. A silicone flap over the recessed USB-C port stops rain from running directly into the most failure-prone connector on the device. Everything else — gaskets, glands, weatherstripping — is incremental on top of these.

What to skip

For completeness: external antenna mods to the phone don't work — the radio is sealed, the impedance match would be wrong, and you'd more likely damage the radio than improve it. Wi-Fi range extenders use standard Wi-Fi, which is the wrong protocol. Microcontroller-based "smart enclosures" with auxiliary radios add complexity without solving any actual problem. Black enclosures fail in the sun. Fuel cells and thermoelectric generators are interesting on paper and useless at $50.

§ 07

Multi-transport coordination

When two bus drivers pass through, the second should pick up what the first missed
Today's behavior. When a transport uploads a bundle to the server it deletes its local copy (Files.delete(path) in TransportToBundleServerManager). Bundles aren't kept after delivery — they're consumed.

A drop box in a heavily-trafficked area accumulates bundles continuously. Multiple bus drivers may pass through during a day. The system should naturally distribute pickup duty — bus driver A takes some bundles, bus driver B takes ones A didn't get to, and over the course of a day everything reaches the server.

The existing protocol gets us most of the way there. The transport-to-server inventory exchange already handles arbitrarily many bundles in a single session. Server-side deduplication silently skips bundles already received. Bundles uploaded by transport A and then also by transport B don't cause any problem — the server takes the first, ignores the second.

What's missing is the drop box's awareness of who has taken what. The drop box should maintain a small ledger:

// per-transport download tracking on drop box
transport_A_id → [bundle_1, bundle_2, bundle_3]   // all confirmed
transport_B_id → [bundle_1, bundle_4, bundle_5]   // 1 yes, 4–5 missed
transport_C_id → [bundle_4, bundle_5, bundle_6]   // next arrival

When transport C arrives, the drop box prioritizes bundles 4 and 5 (no transport has confirmed delivery) over bundle 6 (fresh and equally needs delivery), but still offers everything if there's capacity. Confirmation flows from the existing ACK mechanism — when the originating client eventually receives a return bundle and ACKs it, that ACK propagates back through the system. For older bundles whose ACKs are slow, a heuristic suffices: "N transports have taken it, mark as likely-delivered, deprioritize but don't delete."

The two-hop routing question

The deeper change is server-side. Today, when the server receives a bundle from transport X, it reads the routing.metadata JSON inside the bundle's payload and records which clients X has been encountering. This builds the transport → clients mapping that drives where return bundles get sent.

With a drop box in the chain, the encounter is more accurately described as "client met drop box, drop box met transport X". From the server's perspective today, this looks like transport X met the client directly. That mostly works — return bundles get routed to X, X gives them to the drop box, drop box gives them to the client — but it loses some routing fidelity. If X is unlikely to return to the same drop box but Y is, return bundles for that drop box's clients should preferentially go to Y.

The clean fix is for the drop box to include relay metadata in its own bundles to the server, recording which clients it has seen and which transports have picked up from it. The server's BundleRouting.processClientMetaData() learns the relay topology over time. This is genuinely novel — not every store-and-forward system reasons about its own intermediate nodes — and it's the part of this work that most resembles a publishable research contribution.

§ 08

Build cost & procurement

Concrete numbers as of April 2026 — for budgeting a pilot, not negotiating a contract
Two design decisions drive nearly all of the cost: dual-protocol or Wi-Fi Direct only, and solar or power bank.

The phone-based drop box is genuinely cheap. With a Wi-Fi Direct-only configuration, a complete unit including storage, enclosure, and a power bank lands well under $80. A self-sufficient solar version costs a little more. Adding Wi-Fi Aware support requires a Qualcomm-chipset phone, which roughly doubles the phone cost — Tecno and Itel are predominantly MediaTek, and MediaTek's Wi-Fi Aware driver enablement is uneven enough that you can't rely on it for production.

Notes on procurement

Phones. For Sub-Saharan Africa deployments, the cheapest path is to source used phones locally. Markets in Lagos, Nairobi, and Accra have abundant supply of functional Tecno, Infinix, and Itel devices in the $12–25 range. For US disaster response, used phones from Swappa or Back Market in the same price range work — Moto G or older Samsung A-series at the lower end, Pixel 4a or 5a if dual-protocol is required.

microSD cards. Storage prices are volatile right now due to AI-driven shortages affecting NAND supply10. Budget $18–22 per card to be safe rather than the $15 baseline. Buy from reputable retailers — counterfeit microSDs are common in regional electronics markets and frequently misrepresent capacity.

Power banks. No-name brands work fine for this application — the device draws so little that even a moderately-rated bank lasts 20+ hours. Anker, INIU, Baseus are reliable choices but not necessary for the drop box use case. A regional Chinese brand from the Lagos electronics market at $12–15 is operationally equivalent.

3D printing. If the research group has a printer in-house, filament cost is the only marginal expense (~$2–4 per enclosure). If outsourcing, expect $15–30 per enclosure printed by a service like Hubs or a local maker space, plus turnaround time. PETG is the right material; ASA is better for outdoor UV resistance if available at slightly higher cost ($20–25/kg vs $10–15/kg).

Solar components. The TP4056 + 18650 + 5W panel approach gives the most flexibility. The simpler alternative — a $20 d.light A2T solar lantern with USB out — is operationally equivalent and locally repairable in African deployment regions. Worth considering for pilots where minimizing custom assembly matters more than minimizing per-unit cost.

What scales

At ~$60 per unit for Configuration A, deploying drop boxes at every bus stop in a small city — say 30 stops — is roughly $1,800 in hardware, plus mounting and installation labor. That's well within research budget territory. The constraint becomes operational rather than financial: someone has to maintain them, monitor for theft, swap power banks where solar isn't deployed. The "drop box is cheap, operations are expensive" pattern is consistent with most infrastructure deployments and worth budgeting for explicitly when planning a real pilot.

§ 09

Beyond email — the drop box is application-agnostic

Email is the first app. The drop box stores opaque encrypted bundles and doesn't know what's inside them.
The pattern. Any Android app can integrate with DDD by talking to the BundleClient via the standard DDDClientAdapter ContentProvider IPC.

The K-9 email fork is the first concrete application to ride DDD's transport, but the protocol below is fully generic. A bundle's payload is a directory of ADUs (Application Data Units) keyed by appId/aduId. Every app gets its own namespace. net.discdd.k9 is email; net.discdd.echo is a reflection test app that already exists; future apps for Signal-style messaging, content delivery, even turn-based games would just claim their own appId and the rest of the system handles their data identically.

From the drop box's perspective this is a feature, not a problem. It stores ciphertext keyed by encrypted bundle ID. It cannot decrypt the bundle, which means it cannot tell whether the bundle contains an email or a chess move or a video chunk. It also doesn't need to. The forwarding decision is "do I have this bundle, has the requesting party seen it, when did it arrive" — questions that can be answered without ever knowing what's inside.

This matters for the drop box design because it tells us what not to add. There should be no email-specific logic in the drop box. There should be no application-aware caching. If a future app produces dramatically larger or smaller bundles than email, the drop box should handle both without modification. The only knobs that matter are storage capacity, age-based eviction, and per-transport pickup tracking — all of which are app-agnostic.

How the K-9 integration actually works

The mechanism is worth understanding because it's the template for every future app. The K-9 fork talks to BundleClient through Android's ContentProvider IPC at content://net.discdd.provider.datastoreprovider/messages. The DDDClientAdapter library wraps this with an OutputStream for sending ADUs and an InputStream for receiving them, plus a ContentObserver callback for "new ADUs available." Email content goes through this pipe, gets packaged into the next outgoing bundle, and rides whatever transport (drop box or otherwise) is available. On the server side, a Spring Boot K9DDDAdapter implements ServiceAdapterServiceGrpc, receives the ADUs, and bridges to a real SMTP server.

A future Signal-style messaging app would do exactly the same thing: install alongside BundleClient, use DDDClientAdapter with its own appId, implement a corresponding server-side adapter. The drop box doesn't change at all — it's already storing both apps' bundles, indistinguishable from each other.

§ 10

Open questions and research directions

What we don't know yet, in roughly the order it matters

Near-term (engineering)

  • Wi-Fi Direct + Wi-Fi Aware coexistence on real budget hardware. Both protocols share the radio. Qualcomm chipsets handle the time-slicing well. MediaTek chipsets often don't. We need empirical testing on the exact phone models we'd actually deploy — something like a Tecno Spark 10 or Itel A60 — before committing to dual-protocol on every drop box.
  • How long does a drop box phone actually last? Battery degradation under continuous Wi-Fi Direct GO operation, 24/7, is undocumented. We should run a unit for 6+ months in lab conditions and measure capacity loss, before deploying at scale.
  • How does Android's Doze mode interact with our wake lock? Doze has changed in every Android version since 6.0. The exact behavior on Android 12+ — which is what's in the cheap phones currently shipping — needs verification.

Medium-term (deployment)

  • Drop box density. One per bus stop is the obvious answer. But what's the right cadence in a market town with five stops? Two stops apart? Five? This is a coverage and redundancy question we can't answer from theory.
  • Theft and vandalism rates. Truly unknown until pilots. The hardware is cheap enough that 20% loss might still be acceptable, but operational lessons (what gets stolen, how, when) matter for design iteration.
  • iOS BundleClient. The iOS Wi-Fi Aware framework only landed in iOS 26 and only works on iPhone 12+. There's a path here, but it requires substantial new work and the user base is small in our primary deployment regions. Worth scoping but not prioritizing.

Longer-term (research)

  • Multi-hop without server involvement. Two drop boxes on the same bus route could in principle exchange bundles between themselves whenever the same bus passes both, without the server ever knowing. This is real DTN territory and worth thinking about as the network grows.
  • Wi-Fi Aware ranging as a wakeup trigger. NAN supports approximate distance measurement to nearby devices. A drop box could stay in deepest possible idle, only fully waking when a phone is within 10 meters. The user experience would be indistinguishable from "always on" but the power profile would be dramatically better.
  • NAN-USD (Unsynchronized Service Discovery) for idle announce. A lighter-weight NAN mode standardized for exactly the "I exist, find me when you need me" pattern — no full cluster, no synchronized beacons, just intermittent service discovery broadcasts. Adopted in Matter 1.4.25 and shipped in ESP-IDF examples9. Could substantially lower the drop box's idle power draw further, and is well-aligned with the proximity-trigger idea above.
  • Opportunistic server sync. A drop box at a bus terminal might have intermittent Wi-Fi internet from the terminal itself. When that's available, the drop box could become a quasi-server, syncing bundles directly. This collapses the bus-driver hop entirely for some bundles.
  • Two-hop routing as a published protocol extension. The relay metadata work in § 07 is novel enough that it's worth writing up properly once we have empirical data on whether it actually improves return-bundle delivery rates.

A. Hardware alternatives we considered and rejected

Each of these has appeal in isolation. Each fails on at least one axis that matters for our deployment.

  • GL.iNet pocket router (GL-MT300N-V2). ~$22 on Amazon (verified April 2026). Runs OpenWrt Linux, real external antenna connector option, USB host for storage, low power draw. Genuinely a good piece of hardware. The deal-breaker is that it provides a standard Wi-Fi access point, not Wi-Fi Direct or Wi-Fi Aware. Existing villager phones know how to find a transport via Direct/Aware automatically; they don't know to leave their current Wi-Fi network and join a router's SSID. Working around this means substantial app changes and a worse user experience. Caveat for US deployments: the FCC issued a March 2026 ruling restricting import authorization for consumer routers manufactured outside the US, prompted by compromise concerns around TP-Link devices. GL.iNet is also Chinese-manufactured and not specifically named in available reporting — worth verifying procurement legality before any US disaster-response deployment that relies on this hardware.
  • Raspberry Pi Zero 2W. $15 board, $40+ all-in. Same protocol problem as the router. Additionally: no built-in UI, requires SSH or pre-imaged SD cards for setup, weak built-in radio. The hobbyist tax is real for field deployment.
  • ESP32 microcontroller + SD. $8–15. The cheapest option by far, and meaningfully more capable than we initially credited it with — the original ESP32 plus the S2, C5, C6, and (recently) S3 variants do support Wi-Fi Aware (NAN) natively per Espressif's official ESP-IDF documentation8, including both Service Discovery and Datapath. The NAN duty-cycle behavior also makes idle power draw dramatically lower than a phone's. The ESP32-WROOM-32UE adds an external antenna connector for real range extension. The disqualifying problem isn't the radio — it's the software stack. There is no JVM on the chip, no port of libsignal to embedded C, and the full bundle format (zip-within-zip with double-ratchet encrypted payload) would need a complete from-scratch firmware reimplementation. A separate research project on a "dumb relay" drop box with a custom protocol could make sense — but it's a different and much larger undertaking than deploying drop boxes now.
  • Custom PCB. Premature. The supply chain for cheap Android phones is already optimized for our exact deployment regions. There is no advantage to designing hardware until we've deployed enough phones to know what specifically we'd want to change.
B. Power options beyond the recommended solar lantern setup
  • Grid power, where available. Bus terminals and shops often have outlets. A USB charger and a cable is the cheapest possible setup. Recommended where the deployment site has reliable mains.

  • 5W solar panel + TP4056 charge module + 18650 cells. The recommended self-sufficient setup for African deployments. Roughly $18–30 in parts depending on sourcing, all available from any electronics market that already stocks solar lanterns. A $20 d.light A2T solar lantern with integrated USB out is an operationally equivalent simpler alternative.
  • Larger 20W panel + dedicated charge controller. Overkill for a single drop box, but worth considering for deployments that include heavier loads (e.g., a small status display, or a future drop box network sync).
  • Hand-crank or piezoelectric harvesters. Genuinely don't generate enough power to be useful for our continuous loads. Solar wins by orders of magnitude.
  • Fuel cells. Real technology, $200+ per unit, fuel availability is the killer in our deployment regions. Not viable.

E. K-9 email integration as the template for future apps

The K-9 fork (DDD-thunderbird-android repo) is the first concrete application running on DDD's transport. The integration pattern is worth documenting because it's the template every future app will follow.

The IPC bridge: DDDClientAdapter

Lives in AndroidApps/client-adapter/. Exposes a ContentProvider at content://net.discdd.provider.datastoreprovider/messages. Apps interact with it via:

  • createAduToSend() — returns an OutputStream. Write your app's data to it; close to finalize.
  • receiveAdu(aduId) — returns an InputStream over an incoming ADU's bytes.
  • registerForAduAdditions() — installs a ContentObserver. Callback fires when new ADUs arrive for this app.
  • deleteReceivedAdusUpTo(aduId) — cleanup once your app has processed.
  • MAX_ADU_SIZE = 512 KB. Larger payloads split across multiple ADUs.

The server-side adapter: K9DDDAdapter

Spring Boot service extending ServiceAdapterServiceGrpc. Identifies this app's ADUs by APP_ID = "net.discdd.k9". Receives them from the BundleServer, decrypts and processes, and bridges to a real SMTP server. Outgoing email comes back through the same pipe. Maps DDD client identity to email address via K9ClientIdToEmailMappingRepository.

The control protocol: ControlAdu

Java Properties-format messages prefixed with "# CONTROL", distinguished from user-data ADUs by a magic-byte check. Used for login, register, whoami, and their corresponding ack types. This is how K-9 onboards a new account: send a RegisterControlAdu, wait for RegisterAckControlAdu to come back through the bundle system.

Why this matters for the drop box

The drop box stores opaque encrypted bytes. It doesn't know any of this is happening. A future Signal-style messaging app, a YouTube-style content app, or a turn-based game app would all use the exact same ContentProvider IPC, register their own appId, and ride the same bundles. The drop box treats them all identically — it's just storage and forwarding for whatever ADUs come its way.

The implication for design: there should be no application-specific logic in the drop box code. No email-sized assumptions, no priority-by-app, no caching by content type. Application-agnostic by construction.

F. Why not Trick's approach (Wi-Fi Aware only)?

Trick is a separate research project from the same group, exploring serverless peer-to-peer messaging over Wi-Fi Aware with libsignal-based end-to-end encryption. It's an excellent reference implementation for the Wi-Fi Aware side of our dual-protocol design.

It is, however, intentionally Wi-Fi Aware only. If FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE isn't present, Trick shows an "Unsupported Device" screen and refuses to operate. There's no fallback.

This is the right choice for Trick's research goals — it lets the team focus on what Wi-Fi Aware can do without compromise. It is the wrong choice for DDD because our target population is overwhelmingly on the budget Android devices most likely to lack Wi-Fi Aware support. Wi-Fi Aware is the better protocol where it works; Wi-Fi Direct must remain the universal baseline.

What we should take from Trick: the Wi-Fi Aware session management code in AndroidWifiAwareManager.kt, the deterministic role negotiation pattern, the heartbeat-based reconnection logic, and the empirical performance numbers. What we should not take: the assumption that a single protocol is sufficient.

The drop box implementation breaks naturally into five workstreams that can be tackled in parallel by different people or teams. Each is self-contained enough to start without blocking on the others, with one or two cross-dependencies called out explicitly. Each section below is structured the same way: where the code is today, what to build next, files and components to touch, dependencies, and how you'll know you're done.

How the workstreams relate

The five workstreams below can be picked up independently. WS-1 (drop box mode) is the foundation — anyone running a unit will need this first, and other workstreams test against it. WS-2 (dual protocol) is the largest engineering effort and the most novel; it can develop in parallel with WS-1 and integrate later. WS-3 (client UX) is mostly UI plumbing on the BundleClient side. WS-4 (routing) is the research-flavored work and depends on WS-1 being deployable for real testing. WS-5 (hardware & enclosure) is mechanical and runs entirely outside the codebase — a hardware-oriented contributor or maker can work on this from day one.

None of the workstreams require all the others to be done first. The minimum-viable drop box is WS-1 + WS-5 (mode + physical unit). Everything else improves the result.

Workstream 1 · Foundational · Highest priority

Drop box mode & service hardening

Make BundleTransportService survive forever, headless, on a phone with the screen permanently off. Distinguish it from a moving transport in the discovery layer so clients can see what they're connecting to.

Where we are now

The existing BundleTransportService is already most of the way there. It runs as a foreground service with START_STICKY (auto-restart after kill), creates the Wi-Fi Direct group owner via DDDWifiServer, runs the gRPC RpcServer and a NanoHTTPD FileHttpServer on port 8080, and survives reboots. StorageManager already handles age-based bundle cleanup when storage exceeds a configured limit. The DNS-SD TXT record already advertises ddd, transportId, and recencyBlob.

What's missing is power-management hardening (no WAKE_LOCK, no battery-optimization whitelist), no distinction in the discovery announcement between a moving transport and a stationary drop box, and no automatic enabling of headless operation.

What to build

  1. Add a SharedPreferences flag dropbox_mode readable from BundleTransportService. UI to toggle it in the existing transport app settings.
  2. On service start, if drop box mode is enabled: acquire a PowerManager.PARTIAL_WAKE_LOCK for the lifetime of the service (keeps CPU alive while screen turns off normally); programmatically request battery-optimization whitelisting via the REQUEST_IGNORE_BATTERY_OPTIMIZATIONS intent; ensure no foreground activity holds FLAG_KEEP_SCREEN_ON so the screen times out and stays off. The phone's natural screen-off behavior is what we want — we're not trying to override the display, just keep the service running while the display is dark.
  3. Extend DDDWifiServer.createGroup() TXT record with type=dropbox when in drop box mode, plus an optional location string from preferences.
  4. Wire StorageManager.updateStorage() to run automatically when storage crosses 80% of the configured cap (currently it's manually invoked).
  5. Add a minimal status surface. The phone's notification LED — where present — can be set to an "alive" pattern via the FLAG_SHOW_LIGHTS notification flag, though many modern phones lack a dedicated notification LED. As a fallback, a periodic notification update or the existing foreground-service notification serves as a heartbeat indicator visible through the enclosure's clear panel without requiring screen wake.

None blocking. Can ship without WS-2; runs Wi-Fi Direct alone.

Done when A drop box phone runs unattended for 7+ days with the screen never turning on, survives Doze mode and OOM kills, automatically reclaims storage when full, and presents itself in the BundleClient peer list as a labeled drop box rather than a generic transport.
Workstream 2 · Largest effort · Research-flavored

Dual-protocol discovery — Wi-Fi Aware alongside Wi-Fi Direct

Add Wi-Fi Aware (NAN) publish/subscribe to the transport service so capable phones get the better protocol while everyone else falls back to Wi-Fi Direct. The drop box advertises both; the connecting client picks whichever it supports.

Where we are now

DDD currently uses Wi-Fi Direct exclusively, with the transport always running as group owner via DDDWifiServer. There is no Wi-Fi Aware code in DDD itself.

The Trick research project (a separate codebase from the same group) has a complete working Wi-Fi Aware implementation at composeApp/src/androidMain/.../AndroidWifiAwareManager.kt. It demonstrates: feature availability checking via FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE, simultaneous publish + subscribe after attach, network specifier with PSK, deterministic role negotiation by lexicographic device ID comparison, TCP over the NAN data path, heartbeat monitoring, and reconnection. Performance: 264 ms attach, 977 ms peer discovery, 7.4 ms text latency.

Trick is intentionally Wi-Fi Aware only (it shows an "Unsupported Device" screen if Aware isn't available). DDD must always fall back to Wi-Fi Direct because the target population's budget MediaTek phones often lack Wi-Fi Aware support.

What to build

  1. At BundleTransportService startup, check PackageManager.hasSystemFeature(FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE). If absent, run only the existing Wi-Fi Direct path and exit early.
  2. If present, attach a WifiAwareSession alongside the existing DDDWifiServer. Both publish and subscribe to a service named (for example) "ddd-bundle".
  3. When a peer subscribes to our publish, set up a Wi-Fi Aware data path via WifiAwareNetworkSpecifier, requesting a network through ConnectivityManager. Run the existing gRPC service over that network — the exchange protocol above the network layer is identical to Wi-Fi Direct.
  4. Handle radio coexistence carefully. Both Wi-Fi Direct GO and Wi-Fi Aware share the radio; Qualcomm chipsets handle this cleanly, MediaTek can struggle. Add logging to surface coexistence issues during testing.
  5. Mirror the same logic on the BundleClient side: discover via Aware when available, fall back to Direct.
  6. Document tested phone models (which chipsets cleanly support both protocols simultaneously) so deployment knows what to procure.

A client phone with Wi-Fi Aware support discovers and exchanges via Aware; a client without falls back to Wi-Fi Direct seamlessly. Coexistence behavior is documented for at least three phone models.
Workstream 3 · UI plumbing · Quick win

Client-side exchange progress & drop box labeling

The infrastructure for showing exchange progress and outcome already exists — the broadcast cases are just empty. Wire them up, and use the new type=dropbox TXT field to label drop boxes distinctly in the peer list.

Where we are now

BundleClientService already broadcasts WIFI_DIRECT_CLIENT_EXCHANGE_STARTED and WIFI_DIRECT_CLIENT_EXCHANGE_FINISHED events. BundleClientServiceBroadcastReceiver receives them but the case bodies are empty — there's a commented-out hint about reading the device address from the intent extras, suggesting the wiring was started but not finished.

The BundleExchangeCounts result type already includes uploadStatus() and downloadStatus() with values COMPLETE, FAILED, EMPTY. The data is there; nothing surfaces it to the user.

WifiDirectScreen.kt shows discovered peers with a per-peer "Exchange" button that does work. The text label currently shows just the transport ID.

What to build

  1. Wire the empty broadcast receiver cases to push state updates into WifiDirectViewModel's StateFlow. During an exchange, the ViewModel exposes "exchanging with peer X." On finish, exposes the upload/download counts and statuses.
  2. In WifiDirectScreen.kt, render a progress indicator while a peer's exchange is in progress. On completion, show a clear "✓ Bundle sent" or "✗ Failed" affordance that fades after a few seconds.
  3. Read the type field from each peer's DNS-SD TXT record. If type=dropbox, render the peer with a distinct label and icon — e.g., "Drop Box · Nairobi Bus Stop 3" using the optional location field — versus a generic transport.
  4. For drop box peers specifically, the existing manual exchange button is the primary affordance. Consider whether automatic background exchanges with drop boxes should be more or less aggressive than with moving transports — argument either way; worth user-testing.

Workstream 4 · Research contribution · Routing & encryption-adjacent

Multi-transport coordination & two-hop routing metadata

Make the drop box smart about which bus driver has taken which bundles. Extend routing.metadata to express the two-hop path so the server can route return bundles correctly. The most novel piece of work — and the most likely to be a publishable research contribution.

Where we are now

Today, the system assumes a single hop from client to transport to server. Each client bundle carries a routing.metadata JSON map of {transportId: encounterCount}. The server's BundleRouting.processClientMetaData() reads this and builds a transport → [clientIds] mapping that drives where return bundles get sent.

Bundle deduplication is handled correctly: server skips bundles where the counter is less than or equal to lastReceivedBundleCounter. So multiple transports carrying the same bundle is harmless. TransportToBundleServerManager already handles arbitrary numbers of bundles in one inventory exchange — and after successful upload, deletes the local copy via Files.delete(path).

What's missing: the drop box has no concept of which transport has already taken which bundles, and the server's routing model doesn't know about relay nodes.

What to build · drop box side

  1. Maintain a per-transport download ledger on the drop box: {transportId → [bundleIds]}, persisted to local storage.
  2. When a transport arrives, prioritize bundles that no transport has confirmed downloading. Offer already-handled bundles as redundancy if there's still capacity in the exchange.
  3. Confirmation flows from the existing ACK mechanism — when a return bundle's ACK eventually arrives, the originating client's bundle is fully delivered. For older bundles whose ACKs lag, use a heuristic: "N transports have taken this, mark as likely-delivered, deprioritize further offers but don't delete."
  4. Storage cleanup remains driven by StorageManager for hard limits, but the drop box prefers deleting confirmed-delivered bundles before unconfirmed ones.

What to build · routing metadata side

  1. Define a relay_metadata structure that drop boxes include in their own bundles to the server. This records which clientIds the drop box has seen and which transportIds have picked up from it.
  2. Extend BundleRouting.processClientMetaData() on the server to handle relay entries. The mapping becomes: transport → drop_box → clients where applicable, falling back to the existing direct-transport mapping where no drop box is involved.
  3. When generating return bundles, the server uses the richer routing topology to prefer transports that frequent the drop boxes the originating client uses, rather than just transports that have encountered the client directly.
  4. The protocol extension is backward-compatible: existing transports without relay_metadata behave exactly as today.

Dependencies

Soft dependency on WS-1 — the drop box ledger and relay metadata only matter once drop boxes are deployable in real conditions. This workstream can be designed and prototyped in parallel, but its evaluation requires field data.

Server return-bundle delivery rates measurably improve when drop boxes are in the chain compared to a baseline that ignores relay metadata. Result is publication-worthy.
Workstream 5 · Outside the codebase · Independent

Hardware, enclosure & deployment characterization

Phone selection criteria, 3D-printable mount and enclosure, power system, site survey methodology. Entirely outside the software workstreams — a hardware-oriented contributor or maker can run this from day one without touching any code.

Where we are now

No drop box hardware exists yet. The conversation has settled on used Android phones as the right platform, with a clear preference for Qualcomm chipsets when dual-protocol support is needed. The physical design space has been explored at idea-list depth (~100 ranked items in the Full design appendix) but no enclosure has been designed in CAD.

What to build · phone selection

  1. Identify 3–5 candidate phone models in the $20–80 range that explicitly report FEATURE_WIFI_AWARE. Pixel 4a, Pixel 5a, Moto G Power (specific Snapdragon variant), Galaxy A32 5G are starting candidates.
  2. Test each candidate for stable simultaneous Wi-Fi Direct GO + Wi-Fi Aware operation under load. Document model + Android version + chipset.
  3. Identify a Wi-Fi-Direct-only fallback recommendation for budget deployments where dual protocol isn't needed. Tecno Spark Go and Itel A-series are the obvious candidates given supply chain in target deployment regions.

What to build · enclosure

  1. Design a clamshell PETG enclosure in Fusion 360 or similar. White or light gray for thermal reasons. Modular back panel architecture — same outer shell, swappable backs for wall / pole / eave / solar mounts. Universal phone cradle with model-specific inner trays.
  2. Required features: silicone O-ring gasket channel, GORE-Tex pressure equalization vent, weep hole with mesh insert, recessed USB-C port with silicone flap, inner wall convection ribs, clear front window for status LED visibility, cable management channel.
  3. Mount features: pole clamp with hose-clamp slots (25–50 mm range), 15° forward tilt, anti-rotation pin, keyhole-slot wall plate.
  4. Optional but recommended: aluminum reflector behind phone (curved sheet, +3–5 dB gain in one direction), shade canopy (50 mm overhang), quarter-turn coin-operable latches, tool and maintenance log pocket inside lid.
  5. Print test units, deploy on a test pole, measure thermal behavior over a full sun day, refine.

What to build · power system

  1. Spec two power configurations. Solar: 5–10 W USB panel + TP4056 charge controller + 2–3× 18650 cells, total cost ~$18–30. Power bank swap: 20,000 mAh budget bank, swapped on bus driver's route, ~29 hours runtime per swap.
  2. For the simpler African-market alternative: a $20 d.light A2T solar lantern with USB out is operationally equivalent to the assembled solar setup, locally repairable, and eliminates custom electronics work.
  3. Test runtime under realistic load (Wi-Fi Direct GO active continuously, intermittent exchanges). Verify the phone's battery degradation profile under continuous-charge operation over 30+ days.

What to build · site survey methodology

  1. Develop a one-page checklist for selecting a drop box deployment site: line-of-sight to bus stop waiting area, mounting structure available (pole, eave, wall), shade availability (or design accommodates direct sun), power source if not solar, theft risk assessment.
  2. Document mounting height recommendations (3–4 m optimal where possible) and orientation guidelines (face-out toward primary user direction).
  3. Produce installation instructions clear enough that a non-technical volunteer can deploy a unit in 15 minutes.
